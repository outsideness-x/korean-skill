# Pronunciation and listening

Korean pronunciation work connects three representations:

**orthographic form <-> predictable sound pattern <-> heard or produced chunk.**

Keep them separate. The learner may spell a word correctly but fail to recognize it, or recognize it while pronouncing an unreleased final consonant as if it were an onset.

## Diagnose before correcting

For every pronunciation or listening miss, decide which link failed:

1. letter/block identification;
2. phoneme contrast;
3. syllable boundary or batchim;
4. rule-governed spelling-to-sound change;
5. word segmentation in connected speech;
6. lexical knowledge;
7. working memory or task format;
8. production motor habit.

Use the cheapest discriminating check. If the learner understands the written transcript immediately, the problem is not general comprehension. If they cannot explain the word after seeing it, more audio repetition will not fix the lexical gap.

## High-yield sound domains

Introduce in response to real words and level needs:

- plain, aspirated, and tense obstruent contrasts;
- ㅓ/ㅗ, ㅡ/ㅜ, and other learner-specific vowel contrasts;
- final consonant neutralization and unreleased codas;
- resyllabification before a vowel-initial syllable;
- nasal assimilation;
- liquid/nasal interaction;
- aspiration involving ㅎ;
- palatalization;
- tensification;
- contraction and reduction in common endings and frequent expressions;
- intonation, chunking, and boundary cues.

Do not present every rule at H0. Teach a rule when the learner can decode its spelling and has enough examples for the pattern to have a purpose.

## Minimal contrast loop

1. **Perception baseline:** 6-10 randomized A/B items with no production.
2. **Articulatory cue:** one short instruction about timing, tongue, airflow, or release.
3. **Identification:** new tokens, different speaker where possible.
4. **Production:** word, then short phrase, then new word.
5. **Meaning-bearing transfer:** the contrast changes the word or the listener's interpretation.
6. **Delayed retest:** new voice and new lexical surface.

Keep same/different trials and clean items; otherwise the learner can choose whichever sound was announced as the target.

## Sound-change lesson

Use four columns only when they add clarity:

| Spelling | Morpheme boundary | Connected realization | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 같이 | 같-이 | [가치] | palatalization |

The bracketed form is a listening aid, not a respelling. Require the learner to return to the standard spelling in dictation and writing.

Teach the boundary with the rule. A fresh sound rule tends to over-apply. Pair one positive example with a near-twin where the triggering environment is absent.

## Listening task ladder

1. syllable/word discrimination;
2. short phrase with one known contrast;
3. micro-dialogue with a factual answer;
4. natural short dialogue with correction or change of plan;
5. monologue with sequence and stance;
6. news, lecture, discussion, or professional audio.

Before replaying, ask what the learner needs on the next pass: word boundary, ending, number, negation, speaker attitude, or main idea. Unlimited undirected replay produces familiarity without diagnosis.

When audio tools are available:

- prefer human or high-quality Korean audio with a transcript;
- rotate speakers, age ranges, and careful/natural styles within the learner's goal;
- keep a clean version and, for teaching only, a chunked or slowed version;
- never invent what was said in unclear audio; transcribe uncertainty explicitly;
- do not score a pronunciation solely from automatic speech recognition.

When audio is unavailable, be honest: text can teach the rule and prepare discrimination, but it cannot verify perception or production. Schedule the audio check for later rather than declaring the sound mastered.

## Without a listener

Claude cannot hear audio files or live speech, so it cannot verify the learner's pronunciation. Say this once, before the first production task, and never imply that a recording was evaluated. Then use what is actually available:

1. **Perception is still testable** whenever the learner can play Korean audio: a real recording, or audio the environment can generate. Discrimination, dictation, and listening items need no recording from the learner.
2. **Guided self-monitoring.** The learner records a target and a model, listens back, and answers one narrow question per feature ("At the end of 방, did your tongue touch the roof of your mouth?"). This trains noticing. It is self-report, not evidence.
3. **Dictation-engine proxy.** The learner says isolated minimal-pair words where both options are real words (반/방, 불/풀/뿔, 달/탈/딸) into a Korean speech-to-text engine, such as the phone keyboard's Korean dictation, and pastes the raw output unedited. A consistent misrecognition is a useful flag. A correct recognition proves little, because the engine's language model repairs toward likely words. Record the result as `proxy`, never as verified.
4. **A human listener** (a teacher, a language-exchange partner, a native friend) for any claim that a sound is acceptable. Give the learner the current three targets as a checklist for that listener, and record the listener's verdict as the evidence.

Until step 4 happens, keep pronunciation `not measured` in the skill profile. Perception and production close separately, so perception can close from steps 1-3 while production stays open.

## Speaking feedback

Prioritize by communication cost:

1. lost or added syllables and batchim;
2. contrasts causing word confusion;
3. endings that obscure grammar or social meaning;
4. chunking and prosodic boundaries;
5. accent features that remain intelligible.

When a listener or proxy supplies evidence (see **Without a listener**), return no more than three speaking targets per take. Quote the relevant word or chunk, give one cue, and request a second recording with a changed sentence. Do not ask the learner to imitate an entire paragraph after receiving ten unrelated corrections.

## Closure

A sound pattern closes when it survives a new word, a new speaker, connected speech, and a delayed check. Production and perception close separately. A learner may have one without the other.
