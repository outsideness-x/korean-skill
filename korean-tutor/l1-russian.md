# Russian-speaking learners

Read this when the learner's first language or explanation language is Russian. It lists where Russian helps, as anchors worth using in explanations, and where it misleads, as transfer traps worth probing early. Every item is a hypothesis about this learner until their own evidence confirms it: probe first, as everywhere else in the skill.

## Cyrillic transliteration

Russian-language materials, subtitles, and videos often write Korean in Cyrillic, in the Kontsevich system or an informal spelling. For a Russian speaker this is a more dangerous crutch than Latin romanization, because it reads as real pronunciation. It hides exactly the contrasts Korean depends on:

- it writes ㅓ and ㅗ with the same letter о (and ㅕ, ㅛ with the same ё);
- it renders the plain / aspirated / tense series with Russian letters and digraphs (к or г, кх, кк), which the reader then pronounces with Russian values;
- it writes ㅡ as ы and ㅐ, ㅔ as э, е: close enough to feel solved, different enough to fossilize.

The policy is the same as for romanization (`levels/h0-hangul.md`): first encounter only, then removed. In session zero, ask where the learner has seen Korean in Cyrillic and whether they convert Hangul into Cyrillic in their head while reading; many do and will say so.

## Sounds

| Korean | Russian anchor | Trap |
|---|---|---|
| ㅡ | ы | ㅡ is further back with flat, spread lips and no glide |
| ㅓ / ㅗ | both fall inside Russian о | ㅗ is rounded and higher, toward у; ㅓ is unrounded and more open, between о and а with relaxed lips. The top vowel priority: 거기/고기, 거리/고리 |
| every vowel | none | Russian reduces unstressed vowels (молоко). Korean has no word stress and keeps every syllable full; an imposed stress pattern blurs ㅓ/ㅗ further. Give syllables equal length |
| ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅉ | Russian к, т, п, ч | Russian voiceless stops are unaspirated and fairly tense, so a Korean listener often hears them as the tense series. A Russian speaker's default к tends to land on ㄲ |
| ㅋ ㅌ ㅍ ㅊ | none | these need a clear puff of air, which Russian lacks |
| ㄱ ㄷ ㅂ ㅈ at word start | neither г nor к | weak, slightly breathy, voiceless, and the next vowel starts lower in pitch. The contrast is not voiced/voiceless, which is the only one Russian has |
| finals | final devoicing (код sounds like кот) | a good anchor for "spelling is not sound" (낮, 낯, 낫 all [낟]), but Russian finals are released with a burst; Korean finals are not |
| ㅇ final [ŋ] | none | Russian has no [ŋ]; learners substitute н or нг. The top consonant priority: 방/반, 강/간 |
| ㄹ | a single beat of р; л | the onset is one tap, never a trill; the final is a clear [l], neither the dark Russian hard л nor a palatalized ль |
| ㅎ | not х | Korean ㅎ is a weak breath in the throat, without the friction of х; in connected speech it often fades (전화 often sounds like [저놔]) |
| 시, 씨 | сь, щ region | a helpful anchor as is |
| palatalization | softening before и | a helpful image for 같이 [가치], but Korean palatalization happens only at a morpheme boundary before 이/히; 티 and 디 inside a word stay unchanged |

## Grammar anchors that work

- **Theme and rheme → 은/는 and 이/가.** Russian marks the split with word order and intonation: «Кошка сидит на крыше» (the cat is the theme) versus «На крыше сидит кошка» (the cat is new information). The same contrast is one of the jobs of 고양이는 지붕 위에 있어요 versus 지붕 위에 고양이가 있어요. Teach the boundary in the same lesson: 이/가 is also required where no theme/rheme choice exists (embedded clauses, question words like 누가 and 뭐가, frames such as 있다, 없다, 필요하다), and 은/는 also marks contrast.
- **Experiencer frames.** «Мне нравится кошка», «Мне нужна помощь», «У меня есть время» put the thing in the nominative, just as Korean marks it with 이/가: 고양이가 좋아요, 도움이 필요해요, 시간이 있어요. Contrast «Я люблю кошку» (accusative) with 고양이를 좋아해요. Shown this parallel, Russian speakers usually get these frames quickly.
- **Case intuition.** The idea that a noun carries a role marker transfers: 을/를 is close to the accusative, 에게/한테 to the dative, 의 to the genitive. Boundary: particles are often dropped in speech, and 은/는 is not a case at all.
- **Free word order.** Korean argument scrambling feels natural. Boundary: the predicate stays last, and modifiers always come before the noun.
- **ты / вы.** The idea that grammar encodes the relationship to the listener transfers. Boundary: Korean has several speech levels in its endings (해요체 as the safe default, 합니다체 for formal and public settings, 반말 for close equals and younger people only with consent), and a separate subject honorific -(으)시- about the person being talked about, not the listener. Age and status weigh more than in Russian: someone a Russian speaker would call «ты» can require 해요체.
- **Aspect.** Russian aspect prepares the learner to notice aspect at all. Boundary: the Korean past -았/었- also reports a resulting state: 결혼했어요 means «женат/замужем», not only «женился/вышла замуж», and 입고 있어요 can mean «надевает» or «одет(а)» depending on context.
- **Counters.** «пять человек», «две штуки» are a small precedent for 다섯 명 and 두 개; build number + counter order on it.

## Traps to probe early

- **당신 for «вы».** Dictionaries give 당신 for "you", but in face-to-face speech it is marked: confrontational in an argument, or used between spouses; ads, lyrics, and translations use it generically. Teach name + 씨, titles (선생님, 사장님), kinship terms, or simple omission.
- **Dropped copula.** Russian omits "to be" in the present («Я студент»), so learners produce 저는 학생 as a complete sentence. Treat it like dictionary-form speech: the sentence needs 이에요/예요 or 입니다.
- **Pronoun and plural overproduction.** 저는 in every sentence, and 들 on every plural noun because the Russian plural is obligatory. Korean omits recoverable subjects, and 들 is optional and often absent.
- **где/куда mapped onto 에/에서.** Russian splits location from direction; Korean splits static location and destination (에) from the place where an action happens or movement starts (에서). «Я в школе» 학교에 있어요, «Я учусь в школе» 학교에서 공부해요, «Иду в школу» 학교에 가요, «Иду из школы» 학교에서 와요.
- **Relative clauses after the noun.** «книга, которую я читал» puts the clause after the noun; Korean puts it before: 제가 읽은 책. Learners build the noun first and then look for a «который»; teach the modifier ending as the tool that turns a clause into an adjective.
- **No Hanja background.** Unlike Chinese or Japanese learners, Russian speakers get no free help from Sino-Korean roots. Build the families in `vocabulary-reading.md` explicitly; they pay off early.
- **Loanwords come through English, not Russian.** 컴퓨터, 에너지, 이메일 follow English; a few come through Japanese or German (아르바이트 is a part-time job). Do not expect Russian-shaped forms.

## Diagnostic checklist

Each item is frequent among Russian speakers and cheap to test. Give each one cold probe during placement:

1. ㅓ/ㅗ in reading and in listening (거기/고기, 거리/고리);
2. [ŋ] versus [n] finals in listening (방/반, 강/간);
3. the three-way stop contrast in listening (불/풀/뿔, 달/탈/딸);
4. released finals and vowel reduction in production (needs a listener: `pronunciation-listening.md`, **Without a listener**);
5. 당신 and a dropped copula in a short self-introduction;
6. 에/에서 in a description of where things are and what happens there;
7. relative-clause order in a sentence-combining item;
8. silent Cyrillic transliteration while reading.

## Explanation language

Explain in Russian at H0-K2, as `SKILL.md` prescribes. School grammar terms (подлежащее, сказуемое, падеж, вид глагола) are familiar to most Russian speakers and can be used freely; тема/рема may need a one-line explanation.
